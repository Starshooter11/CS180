Project 1

Images of the Russian Empire:

Colorizing the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection

Background

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii (1863-1944) [Сергей Михайлович Прокудин-Горский, to his Russian friends] was a man convinced that color photography was the future. Winning the Tzar's special permission to travel across the vast Russian Empire and take color photographs of everything he saw, Prokudin-Gorskii photographed quite literally everything, including the only color portrait of Leo Tolstoy. His method was simple, and can be seen in any sort of digital media editing software today: record three exposures of every scene onto a glass plate using a red, a green, and a blue filter. His vision of the future included special projectors to be installed in "multimedia" classrooms all across Russia where the children would be able to learn about their vast country. Unfortunately, his plans were never realized as he left Russia in 1918, right after the revolution, never to return again.

In 1948, Prokudin-Gorskii's RGB plage negatives, capturing the last years of the Russian Empire, were purchased by the Library of Congress. The LoC has recently digitized the negatives and made them available on-line.

The actual task

So of course, this project's goal is to combine the RGB negatives together and make a fully-colored photo!

The basic idea(for small images)

After splitting the images into 3(corresponding to B, G, and R filters), I then crop them to get rid of the border pixels. My code crops 15% off on each side. I also take the B image as my base.

Next, I shift the pixels in the G and R images in every direction between (-20, -20) and (20, 20), compare the pixels to B and, using L2 as the scoring algorithim, and obtain the lowest score(which equates to highest similarity).

Corresponding to the lowest score is the desired shift. Shift G and R accordingly and then layer them right on top of B. Tada! Full colored photos!

The next idea(for larger images)

Obviously, this gets really, really slow for larger images as not only do you check more pixels per direction, but you need to check around 400 directions! What a pain.

The solution? Image pyramid!

Basically, we downsample the image down to a reasonable size, check the 400 directions there, and then upsample it while checking around 4 directions each time

This is because downsampling makes 4 pixels into 1 which can cause errors that propogate up through upsampling.

However, this alone doesn't work for every picture.

This is because something inherent to the negatives themselves can bias or affect the L2 score. Among them are differing contrast ranges, brightness, or even the color balance.

While I don't tackle these problems in this project, it's good to think about why some images are more prone to being distorted than others with just my code alone.
